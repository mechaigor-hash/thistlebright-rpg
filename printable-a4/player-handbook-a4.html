Player Handbook

Character creation, simple rules, kindreds, jobs, spells, gear, and a worked example

Thistlebright Adventures

Scottish fairy myth, high fantasy, safe wonder, and table rules made for brave young players.

How to use this book

  1. Choose who helps as the Guide.
  2. Make a hero in five easy steps.
  3. Use Brave, Kind, and Quick when a roll is exciting.
  4. Keep the story warm: heroes can be muddy, surprised, or silly, but never shamed.
Table feel: point at the picture, ask what the hero notices, then roll only when it is exciting.
For ages 5–7: read choices aloud, let children point to pictures, and use stars or stickers for stats instead of lots of numbers.

What you need

  • One six-sided die.
  • A pencil, character sheet, and three little counters.
  • A grown-up or older sibling Guide.
  • A promise to listen and take turns.

What players say

  • “I help.”
  • “I try the brave thing.”
  • “Can I talk to it?”
  • “I look closer.”
  • “Can my friend help?”
First table promise
Every player gets a turn to say one idea before the dice decide anything.

The easy dice rule

When the answer is obvious, no roll is needed. When everyone leans forward and wonders what might happen, roll one six-sided die and add the hero's matching stat stars.

TotalResultSay it like this
1–2WobbleIt goes wrong in a funny, safe, or messy way.
3–4Yes, but...It works, and there is a tiny cost or choice.
5+Bright successIt works well, and the hero feels proud.
No failure spiral: a wobble changes the scene; it does not stop the adventure.

The three stats

  • Brave — standing tall, protecting, climbing, daring, saying “I can try.”
  • Kind — helping, calming, sharing, talking, understanding feelings.
  • Quick — sneaking, catching, dodging, spotting, balancing.

When not to roll

  • The action is safe and obvious.
  • A child is only describing how they look or feel.
  • The group already found a kind solution.
  • A roll would slow a warm moment.
Helping rule
If another hero clearly helps, add +1 or let the child roll again and choose the better die.
4

Stat creation

Step 1: Pick your best stat

Put ★★ in the thing your hero is best at.

Step 2: Pick your okay stat

Put ★ in the thing your hero can usually do.

Step 3: Leave one blank

Put — in the thing your hero is still learning. This is not bad; it makes stories fun.

Example: Rowan the Beast Friend has Kind ★★, Quick ★, Brave —. Rowan talks gently to foxes, moves carefully, and is still learning to be bold.
Helper question: “Which one sounds most like your hero: brave, kind, or quick?”

If a child wants all ★★

Say: “Every hero is still learning something. Which one will be funny to practise?”

If a child worries about —

Say: “Blank means story chance, not bad. Friends can help you.”

Kindred

Glenfolk

Glenfolk are croft, cottage, castle, and market-lane children with practical hearts. They know how to ask neighbours, find a dry path, and make ordinary things feel brave.

Story gift

Once per adventure, remember a local clue or helpful person.

Look

  • Tartan scarf, muddy boots, treasure pockets.
  • A wooden badge, snack cloth, or tiny family charm.

Good at the table

  • Ask an auntie, shepherd, baker, guard, or gardener for help.
  • Know what a tool is called or where a path should go.
  • Offer practical kindness: a snack, blanket, or fixed latch.

Best first stat

Kind or Quick

First scene idea

A village gate is stuck and everyone has an idea.

Say this

“I know someone who might help.”

Kindred

Thistle Fairy

Thistle Fairies are small bright folk with manners, shimmer, and secret paths. They are excellent at noticing fairy marks that bigger people step over.

Story gift

Once per scene, notice nearby fairy magic.

Look

  • Petal cloak, star freckles, tiny crown.
  • Boots that never quite touch the puddles.

Good at the table

  • Ask flowers, moths, or beetles for tiny gossip.
  • Spot a hidden ring of mushrooms or a polite fairy door.
  • Remember an old rule of fairy manners.

Best first stat

Quick

First scene idea

A secret flower-door opens only after a polite greeting.

Say this

“I bow politely and look for the sparkle.”

Story gift

Look

  • Buttons, string, thimble hat, or polished spoon.

Good at the table

  • Fix a latch, mend a pouch, clean mud from a clue.

Kindred

Heather Giantling

Heather Giantlings are small for giants, huge for fairies, and very gentle. They are best when something heavy, high, or frightening needs a careful friend.

Story gift

Lift, push, or carry one heavy thing safely.

Look

Good at the table

Brave

Adventure job

Thistle Knight

Thistle Knights protect friends and stand bravely at the front. They are not rough or bossy; they make scary moments feel safer for everyone.

Gift

Once per scene, turn a scary moment into a brave one.

Best stat

Brave

Try this

  • Hold a shield or walking stick like a promise.
  • Stand between danger and a friend.
  • Say a brave sentence out loud.

Starter kit

  • Soft shield
  • Promise ribbon
  • Thistle badge

Say this

“I stand tall so my friend can try.”

Team role

This job shines most when it helps another hero have a turn.

Spells, gear, and treasure

Rule: magic helps the story; it does not solve every problem alone.

Safe little spells

  • Glow-pebble: make a small light.
  • Tea-steam: warm cold hands or reveal a breeze.
  • Thistle-tickle: distract a grumpy creature for a moment.
  • Kind whisper: help someone say what they feel.
  • Button-bridge: make a tiny bridge for one small creature.
  • Heather-hush: quiet a noisy room for one careful question.
Spell limit
A spell can help one small problem. Big problems still need friends, ideas, and choices.

Starting gear

  • A snack wrapped in cloth.
  • One useful tool.
  • One pretty charm.
  • One thing your hero drew themselves.
  • A spare ribbon, button, bell, shell, or smooth stone.
  • A tiny notebook for clues or doodles.

Useful tools

ToolUse
LanternSee misty paths.
StringTie, measure, or rescue.
ChalkMark a safe way back.
Wooden cupOffer water, tea, or kindness.

Treasure prompts

FindStory use

BellCalls one fairy friend.
Treasure is story-first: a shiny button can matter more than a bag of coins if it unlocks a promise.

Making a hero: quick checklist

  1. Name your hero.
  2. Pick a kindred.
  3. Pick an adventure job.
  4. Choose stats: ★★, ★, and —.
  5. Pick one gear item and one charm.
  6. Answer: “Who do I want to help?”
Worked example: Rowan Moonbutton is a Selkie-Born Beast Friend. Stats: Kind ★★, Quick ★, Brave —. Gear: oat pouch. Charm: sea-glass button. Rowan wants to help nervous animals.
Grown-up tip: do not quiz a child on rules. Ask what they imagine, then translate that into Brave, Kind, or Quick.

End of session

Ask: “Who did we help?” “What should we draw?” “What promise did we make?”

Tiny advancement

After three adventures, add one sticker beside a gift. The sticker is a memory, not a new complicated rule.

If a player is stuck

Offer two choices: “Do you want to talk kindly, move quickly, or do the brave thing?”

If the group gets loud

Point to the picture, name one sound in the scene, then ask one child what their hero notices.
